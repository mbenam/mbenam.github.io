# Pocket Tracker — Volume 3: The Building Blocks of Famous Music

Ever notice how a lot of songs feel *familiar* the first time you hear them? That's because hit music is built from a small set of shared parts — chord progressions, rhythmic grooves, riff shapes, and sounds — that get reused across thousands of songs. None of those building blocks belong to anyone. They're the common language of music.

This volume teaches that language. Every example here is **original** — you're learning the *recipe*, not copying a dish. Once you know the recipe, you'll start hearing it everywhere, and you'll be able to reverse-engineer your own favorite songs by ear (exactly what Lesson 20 was training you for).

Do Volumes 1–2 first. Rows are in hex (00–0F); beat rows are 00, 04, 08, 0C.

---

# PART A — THE PROGRESSIONS BEHIND EVERYTHING

A **progression** is the sequence of chords a song loops. A handful of them power an astonishing share of popular music. Learn these and you hold the skeleton key.

**How to play any progression in this app:** the Bass plays each chord's **root** on the downbeat of its 4-row group; the Lead arpeggiates the triad (root, skip one, skip one — Lesson 9) or just melodizes over it using in-key notes.

## Lesson 21 — The "Four Chords" (I–V–vi–IV) (25 min)

This is *the* one. More famous singalong songs use this exact progression than any other. In C Major the roots are: **C – G – A – F**.

1. Key = **C Major**. Bass roots, one per 4-row group: **C** (00) · **G** (04) · **A** (08) · **F** (0C). Low octave.
2. Over each, arpeggiate the triad on the Lead:
   - C group → C–E–G
   - G group → G–B–D
   - A group → A–C–E
   - F group → F–A–C
3. Play it looping. That feeling of "I've heard this before"? You have — a thousand times. That's the point.

**Why it works:** it starts at home (I=C), lifts (V=G), turns bittersweet (vi=Am, the sad minor chord), then gently resolves (IV=F) and loops. Emotional round-trip in four chords.

**Variations that also power hits:**
- Start on the sad chord: **A – F – C – G** (vi–IV–I–V) — same four chords, moodier because it opens on the minor.
- **C – F – G – F** — brighter, more "rock."

## Lesson 22 — The Sad/Emotional progressions (20 min)

Switch Scale to **Minor** and the same idea turns cinematic.

- **Am–F–C–G** (roots A–F–C–G): the "epic sad" loop under countless ballads and film trailers.
- **Am–G–F–E** (roots A–G–F–E): a *descending* bassline — each root steps down. Descending progressions feel like sinking, falling, longing. Enormously common in emotional music.
- **The lament (descending bass):** put bass roots that walk straight down the scale — A, G, F, E over four groups — and hold simple lead notes above. The downward pull does all the emotional work.

**You learned:** minor + descending motion = melancholy. It's a *mechanism*, not a mystery.

## Lesson 23 — The Blues / Rock progression (20 min)

The **12-bar blues** is the ancestor of rock, R&B, and a lot of pop. Simplified to fit your loop, it's built on three chords: **I, IV, V** — in C that's **C, F, G**.

- Bass pattern across the loop: **C** (rows 00–07), **F** (08–0B), **C** (0C–0F) — or cycle C-C-F-C for a punchier feel.
- **The signature move:** don't just hold the root — play a **root-and-up** bounce. On the C section alternate C and the note two scale-steps up, back and forth on the beat. That "C… up… C… up…" shuffle *is* the blues bass.
- Use the **Blues** scale in the picker for your lead — it adds the "blue note" that gives blues and rock their grit.

**You learned:** three chords + a bouncing bass + the blues scale = the foundation rock was built on.

# PART B — RIFF ARCHETYPES

A **riff** is a short, repeated musical hook. Famous riffs are individual (and copyrighted), but the *shapes* they're built from are free — and once you know the shapes, you can build endless riffs of your own in the same style.

## Lesson 24 — The driving rock riff (20 min)

The engine of hard rock and metal: a **repeated root note with rhythmic push**, punctuated by a move to a nearby note.

1. Pick a low, aggressive Bass sound (**Acid Bass** or **Synth Bass**, short Release).
2. Hammer the **root** in a syncopated rhythm — e.g. rows 00, 02, 03, 06, 08, 0B. The gaps and repeats create the *drive*; a straight run of notes wouldn't.
3. Every few reps, jump to a nearby in-key note (up to the 4th or 5th) and back. That tension-and-return is the riff's "hook."
4. Double it on the Lead an octave up for power.

**The principle:** rock riffs are mostly *rhythm on one note*, with just enough pitch movement to stay interesting. Groove first, notes second.

## Lesson 25 — The walking bassline (20 min)

The smooth, strolling bass of jazz, blues, and soul. Instead of sitting on the root, the bass **walks** — a steady stream of notes that connect one chord to the next.

1. One bass note per beat row (00, 04, 08, 0C) at minimum — walking bass is *constant*.
2. Between chord roots, step through in-key notes to connect them. Going from a C chord to an F chord? Walk C → D → E → F, one per step. You *arrive* at the next root right as the chord changes.
3. Keep it almost entirely on **bright (in-key)** notes, with the occasional off-key passing note (Lesson 12) for that jazzy slink.

**The principle:** a walking bass is a melody in the low end that always lands on the chord root when the chord changes. Motion between, arrival on the beat.

## Lesson 26 — The arpeggio hook (20 min)

Many famous "melodic" hooks aren't melodies at all — they're **arpeggios** (broken chords) played fast, the notes sparkling up and down.

1. Take any triad (C–E–G). Play it up *and* back down across the steps: C–E–G–E–C–E–G–E...
2. Speed and repetition turn it into a shimmering hook. This is the backbone of a huge amount of electronic, pop, and video-game music.
3. Move the shape to follow a progression (Lesson 21) and the same arpeggio becomes a whole song's worth of hook.
4. Try it on a bright **Saw Lead** or **8-Bit Chiptune** with a bit of **Echo**.

**The principle:** you don't need a clever tune — a chord played as a fast up-and-down pattern *is* a hook. Chiptune, trance, and pop live on this.

## Lesson 27 — The vocal-style lead (20 min)

Instrumental "lead" lines that stick in your head usually mimic how a **voice** sings: short phrases, breaths (gaps), and notes that mostly step rather than leap.

1. Write a 4–6 note phrase in the first 8 rows, then **rest** (silence) for a couple of rows — that's the "breath."
2. Answer it with a related phrase in the back half — maybe the same shape ending on a different note. This is **call-and-response**, how melodies converse.
3. Keep motion mostly **stepwise** (neighbor notes), with one expressive leap for drama. Land the phrase on the root to "finish the sentence."
4. A slower Attack and some Release make it "sing" rather than "blip."

**The principle:** the catchiest leads breathe like a singer. Phrase, breath, answer, resolve.

# PART C — SIGNATURE GROOVES (BY NAME)

These are **rhythmic patterns** — the drum grooves that define whole genres. Rhythms aren't copyrightable, and these named beats are the shared property of music. Build each on the Drum track.

## Lesson 28 — The four named beats every producer knows (30 min)

**1. Boom-Bap (hip-hop):** ~90 bpm.
- KICK on 00 and 07 · SNARE on 04 and 0C · steady HAT on even rows.
- The off-beat kick on 07 is what makes heads nod. Swing it late for extra flavor.

**2. Four-on-the-Floor (house/disco/pop):** ~124 bpm.
- KICK on **every** beat row (00, 04, 08, 0C) · clap/SNARE on 04 and 0C · **off-beat HATs** on the odd rows.
- The steady kick is hypnotic; the off-beat hat gives it lift.

**3. The Backbeat (rock/pop):** any tempo.
- KICK on 00 (and maybe 08) · SNARE hard on 04 and 0C · HAT on every even row.
- The whole feel lives in that snare on 2 and 4 — the "backbeat" you clap along to at concerts.

**4. Dembow (reggaeton/latin pop):** ~95 bpm.
- KICK on 00 and 08 · SNARE on 03, 06, 0B, 0E (the "boom-ch-boom-chick" pattern) · HAT filling around it.
- That snare placement is the entire signature of modern latin pop.

**Try:** build all four with the same tempo and sounds, back to back in patterns 1–4, and hear how *only the rhythm* changes the genre completely.

## Lesson 29 — The fill and the drop (20 min)

Two moves that make a beat feel like a *production*, not a loop.

- **The fill:** in the last 2–4 rows of a pattern, break the groove — a fast run of snares (SNARE on 0C, 0D, 0E, 0F) or alternating kick/snare. It signals "here comes a change" and launches the next section.
- **The drop:** arrange it so a busy pattern is followed by a moment of near-silence, then the full beat slams back. Cut to just bass for a pattern (the "tension"), then hit the full drums+combos (the "release"). This is the single most powerful arrangement move in modern music.
- **The build:** across a pattern, add more hi-hats each time it loops (or raise the Cutoff sweep, Lesson 18) so energy *rises* into the drop.

**You learned:** fills announce change; drops deliver it; builds create the anticipation that makes a drop land.

# PART D — "SOUNDS LIKE" SOUND DESIGN

Getting a sound "in the style of" a genre — no copying, just the recipe. Build these from the sliders (Lesson 6/18).

## Lesson 30 — Iconic sound recipes (25 min)

- **The 808 sub-bass (trap/hip-hop):** Sine wave · low Cutoff · long-ish Decay/Release · Sustain low. Deep, round, felt-more-than-heard. Play it *low*.
- **The supersaw festival lead (EDM):** Saw wave · Cutoff high · medium Release · a touch of LFO · **Echo on**. Big, bright, euphoric. Play chords as fast arpeggios.
- **The lo-fi piano (chill/study beats):** Triangle or Sine · soft Attack · medium Release · Cutoff rolled back for that "warm, dusty" muffle. Keep it gentle and a little sad (Minor scale).
- **The acid squelch (techno):** Saw wave · **sweep the Cutoff live** while an LFO wobbles · short Release. The moving filter *is* the sound.
- **The pluck (pop/tropical):** Square or Saw · Attack at zero · very short Decay · Sustain near zero. Snappy and percussive — great for bouncy melodies.
- **The warm pad (ballads/ambient):** Sine/Triangle · long Attack · high Sustain · long Release · gentle LFO. Fills the background with emotion.

**Habit:** when you nail a sound, write down its six slider positions. That's your custom preset — the pros keep libraries of exactly these.

## Lesson 31 — Put it together: reverse-engineer by ear (ongoing)

Now you have the vocabulary. Here's how to figure out a song you love — legally and educationally, for your own learning:

1. **Find the tempo.** Tap your foot to the song, match it with the app's tempo until they lock.
2. **Get the drum groove.** Which named beat (Lesson 28) is it closest to? Start there, adjust by ear.
3. **Find the root.** Hum the bass. Hunt for its home note on the keyboard — that's your key. Set the scale helper to match (major if bright, minor if moody).
4. **Trace the progression.** Does it feel like the Four Chords? A descending lament? Test the common ones from Part A against what you hear.
5. **Catch the hook's shape.** Is it a riff on one note, an arpeggio, or a vocal-style phrase? You know all three now.
6. **Rebuild it in your own way.** Don't copy note-for-note — capture the *feel* with your own notes. You'll learn ten times more, and what you make is yours.

**The truth about famous music:** almost none of it is complicated. It's a handful of these building blocks, arranged with taste and confidence. You now have the blocks. The rest is play.

## Volume 3 cheat sheet

- **Four Chords (I–V–vi–IV):** C–G–A–F. The most-used progression in pop. Learn it cold.
- **Sad = minor + descending bass.** Am–F–C–G, or roots walking downward.
- **Blues/rock = 3 chords (I–IV–V)** + a bouncing bass + the blues scale.
- **Rock riff = rhythm on one note** with small pitch moves. Groove first.
- **Walking bass = constant motion, arriving on the root** at each chord change.
- **Arpeggio hook = a chord played fast up-and-down.** A whole style on its own.
- **Vocal lead = short phrases + breaths + stepwise motion**, resolving home.
- **Named beats:** boom-bap, four-on-the-floor, backbeat, dembow. Same kit, different rhythm = different genre.
- **Fill → build → drop** is the arc that makes a loop feel produced.
- **Sound "like" a genre** by recipe, not by copying — the sliders are the same everywhere.
- **Famous songs are simple.** These blocks + taste = hits.
