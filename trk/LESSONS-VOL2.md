# Pocket Tracker — Volume 2: Beyond the Basics

Volume 1 got you making complete tracks. Volume 2 makes them sound *intentional* — real chords, real grooves, real genres, and the deeper tricks the app can do. Do Volume 1 first; everything here assumes you're comfortable placing notes, using the scale helper, and arranging patterns.

Three threads run through these lessons, braided together:
- **Musical depth** — chords, rhythm, and why things feel the way they do.
- **Genre recipes** — step-by-step beats you can build right now.
- **App mastery** — getting more out of three tracks than seems possible.

# PART A — MUSICAL DEPTH

## Lesson 9 — Chords, faked with one melody line (25 min)

You have one Lead track, not three — so you can't hold three notes at once. But you can *imply* a chord by playing its notes quickly one after another. This is called an **arpeggio**, and it's everywhere in music.

**What a chord is:** three notes from your scale, stacked. The easiest is the **triad** — root, skip one, skip one. In C Major: **C – E – G**. That's a C chord.

1. Key = C Major. On the **Lead** track, place: row 00 = **C**, row 01 = **E**, row 02 = **G**, row 03 = **C** (higher octave).
2. Play. Fast enough, your ear hears it as one warm chord, not four notes.
3. Now the trick that sounds pro: keep that C-E-G shape but **slide it** to start on other scale notes.
   - Rows 04–07: start on **F** → F–A–C (an F chord)
   - Rows 08–0B: start on **G** → G–B–D (a G chord)
   - Rows 0C–0F: back to **C** → C–E–G

You just played **C – F – G – C**, one of the most common chord progressions in all of pop. Put your Lesson-4 bass roots under each change (C, F, G, C) and it locks together.

**You learned:** a chord is a stack of every-other scale note; arpeggios fake chords on a single track; moving the same shape gives you a progression.

## Lesson 10 — Progressions that carry a song (20 min)

A **progression** is the sequence of chords a song loops. The bass plays the chord's root; the lead arpeggiates or melodizes over it. Try these — each is four chords, one per group of 4 rows. Set your bass to the **root** of each.

- **The "sad but pretty" (Minor):** switch Scale to **Minor**. Roots: **A – F – C – G** (Am–F–C–G). Millions of songs.
- **The "50s classic":** C – A – F – G (root motion C, A, F, G).
- **The "epic build":** C – G – A – F. Feels like it's rising even when it isn't.

For each: put the bass root on the downbeat of each 4-row group, then noodle a lead melody using only **bright (in-key)** notes over the top. The chords do the emotional work; your melody just rides them.

**You learned:** the bass defines the chord, the progression defines the mood, and a simple melody over good chords sounds better than a fancy melody over none.

## Lesson 11 — Rhythm that grooves, not marches (20 min)

Notes on every beat sound stiff. **Syncopation** — putting notes *between* the beats — is what makes music feel human and funky.

1. Take a plain bassline: roots on 00, 04, 08, 0C. Sounds like a march.
2. Move some of them **off** the beat: try root on 00, then **03**, then **08**, then **0B**. Same notes, suddenly it bounces.
3. On drums, add a kick on an *off* row (like **07** or **0E**). That "extra" kick where you don't expect it is the secret of funk, house, and hip-hop.
4. **Ghost notes:** add very sparse hi-hats on odd rows between your main hats. They fill without cluttering.

**The "leave a hole" rule:** the most powerful move in rhythm is *silence* on a beat you'd expect to be filled. Delete a note where the ear expects one and the groove breathes.

**You learned:** off-beat placement creates groove; a missing note can hit harder than an added one.

## Lesson 12 — Tension and release (15 min)

Music feels good because it leaves "home" and comes back. You've felt this — ending on the root feels resolved. Now use it on purpose.

- **Away and back:** build a melody that wanders to a far, tense note (a bright note *not* the root, like the 7th — B in C Major) and only resolves to C at the very end of the loop. The delay makes the landing satisfying.
- **The off-key passing note:** deliberately place ONE dimmed (off-key) note *between* two in-key notes, on a fast step. It sounds spicy in passing but wrong if you land on it. This is a "blue note" — the amber dot is warning you correctly, and you're overriding it on purpose.
- **Drop and lift:** in one pattern, strip everything to just bass (tension); in the next, slam the full drums back in (release).

**You learned:** tension is distance from home; release is the return. Off-key notes work *in motion*, not at rest.

# PART B — GENRE RECIPES

Each recipe is a starting point, not a cage. Build it, play it, then break it. Rows are in hex (00–0F); "beat rows" are 00, 04, 08, 0C.

## Lesson 13 — Hip-Hop / Boom-Bap (20 min)

Slow, heavy, head-nod music. **Tempo ~85–95.**

- **Drums:** KICK on 00 and 07 (that second kick is off-beat — crucial). SNARE on 04 and 0C. HAT on every even row; make 00 a **KICK+HAT** and 04/0C **SNARE+HAT**.
- **Bass:** deep sound (**808 Sub Bass**). Root on 00 and 07 to follow those kicks. Keep it low and sparse.
- **Lead:** a short, moody 3–4 note phrase in **Minor** scale, lots of space. Load **E-Piano** or **Harp**.
- **Feel:** laid-back. Don't fill every gap. The space *is* the style.

## Lesson 14 — House / Four-on-the-Floor (20 min)

Dance music. Steady, hypnotic, driving. **Tempo ~120–128.**

- **Drums:** KICK on **every** beat row — 00, 04, 08, 0C (that's "four on the floor"). SNARE (or clap) on 04 and 0C stacked with the kick using **SNARE+HAT**? No — keep snare separate: SNARE on 04, 0C. HAT on all the **odd** rows (01, 03, 05...) — the off-beat hat is the house signature.
- **Bass:** a bouncy off-beat bassline. Put bass notes on the odd rows *between* kicks (01, 03, 05...). Use **Synth Bass** or **Acid Bass**, short Release so it's punchy.
- **Lead:** a repeating stab — one chord arpeggio (Lesson 9) hit on the beats. **Dream Pad** for smooth, **Saw Lead** for energy.
- **Feel:** relentless and even. Repetition is the point.

## Lesson 15 — Chiptune / 8-Bit (20 min)

Video-game music. Bright, bouncy, nostalgic. **Tempo ~130–150.**

- **Sounds:** Lead = **8-Bit Chiptune** or **Square Lead**. Bass = **Square Lead** in a low octave. This is the authentic NES palette.
- **Drums:** chiptune often *fakes* drums with noise — but you have real ones. KICK on 00/08, SNARE on 04/0C, fast HATs. Keep it snappy.
- **Bass:** fast and busy — an arpeggiated root-fifth pattern (C, then G, repeating) on lots of steps. Old consoles kept bass moving constantly.
- **Lead:** a fast, happy **Major** melody with quick runs of stepping notes. Use short Attack and short Release for that "blippy" pluck.
- **Trick:** long, fast arpeggios (Lesson 9) are the chiptune signature — they faked chords because the hardware only had a few voices, exactly like your single Lead track.

## Lesson 16 — Ambient / Cinematic (20 min)

Slow, spacious, emotional. Almost no drums. **Tempo ~70–90.**

- **Sounds:** Lead = **Dream Pad** or **Dark Pad** with **long Attack** (slow swell) and **long Release** (long tails). Turn **Echo** on with high Mix.
- **Bass:** one low root note held for a whole pattern. Change it once per pattern to shift the mood.
- **Lead:** very few notes — 2 or 3 across all 16 steps, all in-key, lots of silence. Let the echo fill the gaps.
- **Drums:** none, or a single soft HAT every 4 rows. Restraint is everything.
- **Feel:** if it sounds "too empty," you're probably close. Remove more.

# PART C — APP MASTERY

## Lesson 17 — Getting big sound from three tracks (25 min)

You have Bass, Lead, Drum. Here's how to make three feel like a full band.

- **Octave layering the illusion of two leads:** write your melody, then in the next pattern copy it and shift key notes up an octave (**Higher**). Alternating patterns makes one line feel like it has range.
- **Call and response:** let the Lead play a phrase in the first 8 rows, then go *silent* while a drum fill answers in the last 8. Two "instruments" trading, using one track and time.
- **Bass doubling as melody:** in a sparse section, let the bass play an actual tune (not just roots) while the lead rests. Now the low end carries the song.
- **The combo pads as texture:** KICK+HAT and SNARE+HAT don't just save a row — they thicken the hit. Use them on the main beats and plain HAT on the in-betweens so the downbeats feel heavier.

## Lesson 18 — Sound design recipes (25 min)

Presets are starting points. Here's how to *build* a sound from the sliders (Lesson 6 taught what each does — now combine them).

- **A punchy pluck:** Saw or Square wave · Attack all the way down · short Decay · Sustain near 0 · short Release. Great for bass and stabs.
- **A slow pad:** Sine or Triangle · long Attack · high Sustain · long Release · Cutoff low-ish for warmth. Turn on a slow, shallow LFO for life.
- **A screaming lead:** Saw wave · Cutoff high · a touch of LFO depth · medium Release. Add Echo.
- **A wobble bass:** Triangle or Saw · low Cutoff · **high LFO depth**, medium-low LFO rate. That's the "wub."
- **The filter sweep (do this live):** set Cutoff low, press play, and slowly drag Cutoff up over a few loops. The track "opens up." It's the most-used move in electronic music and you can perform it by hand.

**Habit:** when you find a sound you love, note the five slider positions somewhere. That's your personal preset.

## Lesson 19 — Arranging a full track (30 min)

Four patterns is enough for a real song if you use them as *sections*. A classic pop arrangement, mapped to your 4 patterns and All-patterns loop mode:

1. **Pattern 1 — Intro/Verse:** bass + lead, light drums. Establishes the mood.
2. **Pattern 2 — Chorus:** full drums (combos on the beats), bass locked to kick, the "big" version of your melody. This should feel like a lift from Pattern 1 — that contrast is the payoff.
3. **Pattern 3 — Variation:** the chorus groove but change *one* thing — a new melody, a busier bass, a drum fill. Keeps the ear interested.
4. **Pattern 4 — Breakdown:** strip it back — drop drums, leave bass + a lonely lead. Builds anticipation before it loops back to the full Pattern 2.

**The rule of contrast:** every section should differ from its neighbor in *density* (how full it is). Full → sparse → full is what makes a song feel like it's going somewhere. Save often.

## Lesson 20 — Develop your ear (ongoing)

The real skill isn't the app — it's hearing. Small daily habits:

- **Steal one bar.** Pick a song you love, find its main beat, and recreate just the kick/snare/hat pattern by ear. You'll be shocked how simple most are.
- **Name what you hear.** When something moves you, ask: is it the bass? the chord change? the space? Naming it lets you reuse it.
- **Copy, then mutate.** Rebuild a groove you like, then change one thing at a time until it's yours.
- **Finish things.** A finished 30-second loop teaches more than ten abandoned ideas. Save it, come back, improve it.
- **Constraints breed creativity.** Three tracks and 16 steps isn't a limitation — it's the same box that made chiptune classics. Master the box before wishing for a bigger one.

## Volume 2 cheat sheet

- **Chord = every-other scale note stacked** (C–E–G). Arpeggiate it to fake it on one track.
- **Progression = the chord sequence**; bass plays the roots. C–F–G–C and Am–F–C–G are money.
- **Groove = off-beat notes + deliberate silence.** Marches are on-beat; funk is between.
- **Tension = distance from the root**; resolve to home to feel satisfied. Off-key notes work *passing through*, not landing.
- **Genre = tempo + drum placement + sound choice.** Same grid, different rules.
- **Contrast in density** is what turns 4 loops into a song.
- **Three tracks is plenty.** Trade phrases, layer octaves, use silence.
