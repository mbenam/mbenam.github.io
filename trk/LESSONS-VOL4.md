# Pocket Tracker — Volume 4: Making It Sound Good, Making It Mean Something

The first three volumes taught you to make music that's *correct* — right notes, real grooves, solid structure. This volume covers the two things that separate a beginner's loop from a track that gives people chills: **making it sound clean and finished**, and **making it feel like something**.

These are the skills nobody teaches beginners, because they're harder to write down than "kick on beat 1." But they're what actually matters. Do Volumes 1–3 first.

---

# PART A — MIXING & FINISHING

"Mixing" sounds technical, but with three tracks it comes down to one idea: **give each sound its own space so they don't fight.** A muddy track is three things crammed into the same place. A clean track is three things you can each hear clearly.

## Lesson 32 — Why your track sounds "muddy" (20 min)

Muddiness = too much happening in the same frequency range at the same time. The fix is *separation*. Play one of your finished loops and listen for these problems:

- **Bass and lead colliding.** If your bass is high and your lead is low, they blur into each other. Fix: keep **bass low** (octave 2–3, use *Lower*) and **lead high** (octave 4–5, use *Higher*). Put real distance between them. This one change fixes most mud.
- **Everything playing at once, always.** If all three tracks hit every beat, nothing stands out. Fix: let them take turns. When the lead is busy, thin the drums. When the bass walks, simplify the lead.
- **Too-bright sounds stacking.** Three buzzy saw sounds together = harsh noise. Fix: vary the waveforms — a sine bass under a saw lead reads clearly; two saws don't.

**The test:** can you hum each of the three parts back separately after one listen? If yes, it's clean. If they blur, something needs to move.

## Lesson 33 — Using the filter to carve space (20 min)

The **Cutoff** slider isn't just "brightness" — it's your main mixing tool. It decides how much space a sound takes up.

1. On the **Bass**, roll the Cutoff *down* (darker). Bass doesn't need brightness — it needs to sit low and stay out of the lead's way. A dark bass leaves room up top.
2. On the **Lead**, keep the Cutoff *up* (brighter) so it cuts through and grabs attention. The lead is the star; let it shine on top.
3. Now they occupy different spaces — low-and-dark vs. high-and-bright — and both are clearly audible.

**The principle:** dark sounds sink to the bottom, bright sounds float to the top. Use Cutoff to *assign each track a floor in the building* so they're not stacked in one room.

## Lesson 34 — When NOT to add things (15 min)

Beginners add. Producers subtract. The most common mistake is a track that's *too full*.

- **The mute test:** play your loop and mentally remove one track. If it sounds *better* or *clearer* without it in a section — leave it out there. Silence from one track is space for another.
- **The "one star" rule:** in any given moment, one track should be the focus. If the bass is doing something cool, the lead should get out of its way, not compete.
- **Density over time:** a track that's full the whole way through is exhausting and flat. The ear needs contrast (Volume 2, Lesson 19). Empty sections make full sections hit harder.

**The principle:** every sound you add costs clarity. Add only what earns its place. When in doubt, take something *out*.

## Lesson 35 — Depth with echo and dynamics (20 min)

Flat tracks have everything at the same "distance." Real tracks have foreground and background — depth.

- **Echo = distance.** A sound with lots of Echo (high Mix) feels *far away* and spacious; a dry sound feels *close and present*. Put Echo on the lead to place it in a big room; keep the bass and kick dry so they stay up front and punchy. Never drown the kick in echo — it needs to be tight.
- **Loud and soft matter.** If every note is the same intensity, it's robotic. Your ADSR **Attack** and **Sustain** control this — a mix of punchy short notes and softer sustained ones creates life. (Even just alternating notes and rests, Volume 2 Lesson 11, does this.)
- **Space around the important note.** The note right before a big moment should have *room* — a beat of silence before the drop makes the drop enormous.

**The principle:** depth comes from contrast — near vs. far (echo), loud vs. soft, busy vs. still.

## Lesson 36 — When is it done? (15 min)

The hardest question. Beginners either quit too early (a bare loop) or fiddle forever. Here's how to know.

**It's NOT done if:**
- It's a single 16-step loop with no variation — that's a sketch, not a song. Give it at least a second section.
- Two tracks are fighting and you can't hear one clearly — fix the mix first.
- You're bored 8 seconds in — it needs contrast, a fill, or a drop.

**It IS done when:**
- It has at least two sections that *contrast* (full vs. sparse).
- You can hear all three parts clearly.
- It has a beginning (something establishes) and some kind of arc (it goes somewhere and comes back).
- You've listened three times and only want to change tiny things — that's the "good enough to finish" signal. Chasing perfect kills more tracks than any other habit.

**The rule that matters most:** *finish it.* A finished imperfect track teaches you more than a perfect unfinished one. Save it, name it, move on. Come back next week with fresh ears — you'll hear exactly what to improve, and you'll improve faster by finishing ten okay tracks than by polishing one forever.

# PART B — EMOTION & STORYTELLING

Music's real job isn't correct notes — it's making a listener *feel* something. This is learnable. Emotions in music come from specific, nameable choices you already have the tools for.

## Lesson 37 — The emotional toolkit (20 min)

You have direct control over mood. Here's the map — change one variable, change the feeling:

- **Major scale = bright, happy, resolved.** Minor scale = sad, tense, serious. This is the biggest single lever. Same melody, flip the scale, opposite emotion.
- **Fast tempo = energy, urgency, joy or panic.** Slow tempo = calm, sadness, weight. 
- **High notes = light, hopeful, delicate.** Low notes = dark, powerful, heavy.
- **Lots of space = calm, lonely, spacious.** Densely packed notes = intense, anxious, exciting.
- **Consonance (in-key, resolved) = comfort.** Dissonance (off-key, unresolved) = unease, longing.

**Exercise:** write one simple 8-note melody. Now make four versions — (1) major + fast + high, (2) minor + slow + low, (3) major + slow, (4) minor + fast. Same notes, four completely different emotions. *That's* composition.

## Lesson 38 — Writing a specific feeling on purpose (25 min)

Don't wait for inspiration — engineer the emotion. Pick a target and build toward it:

- **"Triumphant / heroic":** Major scale · medium-fast tempo · a lead that *rises* (notes climbing upward) · big brass or saw sound · strong four-on-the-floor or backbeat drums · land hard on the root. Rising motion = victory.
- **"Sad / longing":** Minor scale · slow · a descending bassline (Lesson 22) · soft pad or piano sound · lots of space · a melody that reaches up but falls back down without fully resolving. Falling motion + unresolved = yearning.
- **"Tense / scary":** Minor · off-key dissonant notes held uncomfortably · low rumbling bass · sparse, unpredictable drum hits · no comfortable resolution. Denying the ear "home" creates dread.
- **"Peaceful / dreamy":** Major or pentatonic · slow · warm pad with long attack · heavy echo · very few notes, lots of silence · gentle, no sharp attacks. Softness everywhere.

**The method:** name the emotion first, then pull the right levers from Lesson 37. Working backward from a feeling is how film composers work.

## Lesson 39 — Tension and the emotional arc (25 min)

A song isn't one emotion held flat — it's a *journey* between them. The magic is in the *change*.

Think of your 4 patterns as a story with a shape:

1. **Establish (Pattern 1):** set the mood — calm, sparse. This is "once upon a time." Don't give everything away.
2. **Build (Pattern 2):** add energy — more drums, busier bass, the melody grows. Tension rising.
3. **Peak (Pattern 3):** the emotional high point — fullest, loudest, most resolved *or* most intense. The payoff everything led to.
4. **Release (Pattern 4):** come down — strip back, breathe, resolve home. The "ever after."

**The single most powerful emotional move — the drop:** right before your peak, cut almost everything to near-silence for a moment (just a lonely bass note or one held pad). Then slam the full arrangement in. That contrast — emptiness then fullness — is *the* thing that gives people chills. It works because you *denied* them energy, then delivered it. Tension, then release.

**Exercise:** take a finished loop and arrange it across 4 patterns as establish → build → peak → release, with a one-beat drop before the peak. Play it in All-Patterns mode. That's no longer a loop — it's a piece with a beginning, a climb, a summit, and a landing.

## Lesson 40 — Music for a scene or story (25 min)

*(This one's for you specifically — pairing music with games or stories.)*

Music tells the listener how to feel about what they're seeing. Practice writing to a *purpose*:

- **A peaceful village / hub:** Major or pentatonic, medium-slow, warm and simple, loops gently without demanding attention. It should be pleasant to hear for the 100th time — restraint and repetition are features, not bugs.
- **A tense dungeon / danger:** Minor, sparse, low, unpredictable. Leave lots of space so silence itself feels threatening. Less is scarier than more.
- **A boss fight / action:** fast, driving drums (four-on-the-floor or a hard backbeat), aggressive saw bass riff (Lesson 24), an urgent minor lead. High energy, relentless, room for adrenaline.
- **A victory / reward:** short, bright, major, rising, triumphant — a musical "you did it!" Land on the root, big and resolved.
- **An emotional cutscene:** slow, minor, a simple vocal-style lead (Lesson 27) over a descending progression, heavy echo. Let it breathe.

**The principle for functional music:** it serves the moment, not the composer's ego. Background music that loops for hours needs to be *unobtrusive and repeatable*; a one-time emotional beat can be dramatic. Match the music's demands to how it'll be used.

**Exercise:** pick three "scenes" (a calm morning, a chase, a sad goodbye) and write a short loop for each. Play them for someone *without* saying which is which — if they can guess the scene, your music is telling the story.

## Volume 4 cheat sheet

**Mixing & finishing:**
- **Mud = collision.** Keep bass low+dark, lead high+bright — separation fixes most problems.
- **Cutoff assigns space:** dark sinks, bright floats. Use it to keep tracks out of each other's way.
- **Subtract, don't add.** One star per moment. Empty space makes full sections hit.
- **Echo = distance.** Wet = far, dry = close. Keep the kick dry and tight.
- **Finish it.** Two contrasting sections, all parts audible, an arc — then save and move on. Ten finished > one perfect.

**Emotion & storytelling:**
- **The levers:** major/minor, fast/slow, high/low, dense/sparse, resolved/unresolved. Change one, change the feeling.
- **Engineer emotion:** name the feeling first, then pull the levers toward it.
- **A song is a journey:** establish → build → peak → release.
- **The drop** (silence then fullness) is the chills-giving move. Deny, then deliver.
- **Functional music serves the moment** — repeatable for background, dramatic for one-time beats.
- **The real test:** can a listener feel what you intended without being told? If yes, you're a composer.
